History

How Jiu-Jitsu Got Here

1914
Mitsuyo Maeda goes to Brazil
Mitsuyo Maeda (Conde Koma), a Kodokan judo man, travels the world and ends up in Brazil.
Around 1917
Meeting the Gracie family
Carlos Gracie learns jiu-jitsu from Mitsuyo Maeda. Later, together with his younger brother Helio, he develops it in his own way, and Brazilian jiu-jitsu is born.
1925
The Gracie Academy opens
A gym opens in Rio de Janeiro, and the family keeps turning the art into a system.
1993
The first UFC
An openweight, no-time-limit tournament to decide "which martial art is the strongest." Royce Gracie (180 cm, about 80 kg), small even by family standards, beat a boxer, a shootfighter and a savate world champion, all by submission, in under 5 minutes total, and won it all. "The small can beat the big with technique" became common knowledge around the world, and the jiu-jitsu boom took off.
Mid-1990s
Jiu-jitsu lands in Japan
With the vale tudo boom, excitement for jiu-jitsu grew in Japan too, and gyms started popping up around the country.
1996
A national federation is born
Japan's first Brazilian jiu-jitsu federation forms, and a national tournament structure starts to take shape.
2013
The sports jiu-jitsu tournament system starts up
The tournament system of the Asian federation (ASJJF) goes live. Tournaments under SJJJF (Sports Jiu-Jitsu Japan Federation) spread across the country.
2018 onward
The number of gyms keeps climbing
JBJJF member gyms grew from 253 in 2018 to 390 in 2026, about 1.5x in 8 years.
2026
SJJIF World Championship comes to Yoyogi, Tokyo
The world championship arrives in Japan. 1,455 entries from Japan, and 75 domestic tournaments that year.

Years to promotion: we looked at 19,061 competitors who appear in domestic tournament entry lists (2016–2026, 423 tournaments, 95,527 total entries), leaving out 624 with conflicting data that seem to be different people with the same name. For each one, we took the gap between their first tournament year at one belt and their first tournament year at the next. "White belt → Black belt" is measured only from the 44 people confirmed to have competed at white, at black, and at least twice at the belts in between.
These are estimates from tournament records, not exact promotion dates, and they come from the dedicated people who keep competing. People generally say white to black takes 8 to 10 years, and the measured median of 7.5 years fits with that. The belt distribution combines 2026 tournament entries from Japan's two federation systems (24 sports jiu-jitsu tournaments + 15 IBJJF-system tournaments, kids excluded). At regular tournaments, black belts make up 2 to 6% of competitors, but at the world championship level they rise to around 15% and white belts drop. The higher the level of the tournament, the more the upper belts gather, and the numbers show exactly that. "Blue belt → Black belt" had few directly tracked cases, so it's a stacked estimate adding the measured blue → purple and purple → black figures.
