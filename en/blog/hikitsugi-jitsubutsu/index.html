My friend showed me the real birth-leave handover guide

2026.10.20 · Ai Murata (Manager)

Hi, this is Ai Murata.

The day before yesterday, I wrote about my friend making a handover guide with Claude for while she was giving birth. Then she shared the real thing with me: "This is what I actually made."

I was blown away. A 9-page handover notebook, plus a separate 2-page medicine summary. It was far more serious than I'd imagined.

Family names, schools, phone numbers and so on obviously can't go on a blog, so I've hidden all of that. I'll just show you what sections it had and what makes it so good. I hope it helps if you'd like to make your own.

The 12 sections

The handover notebook was laid out in this order, from the top.

  1. Read this firstYou don't have to be perfect, plus 3 rules to keep
  2. Kids' basic infoSchool and daycare, teachers, allergies, signs they're getting sick
  3. Emergency contactsGrandparents, hospital, family doctor, school, after-school care, daycare
  4. A typical weekdayFrom waking up at 7 to bedtime
  5. Morning prep, things to bring, drop-off and pickupWhat to bring on which day, where things are kept
  6. MealsFavorites, dislikes, the usual breakfasts
  7. Bath and bedtimeBedtime, picture books, waking up at night
  8. Emotional careSigns of a bad mood or anxiety
  9. Things to help while Mom is awayDaily video calls, letters, little rewards
  10. Contacting school and daycareHow to report absences and by what time
  11. When they're sickWhen to keep them home, who to call at night, where the medicine is
  12. Bare-minimum housework and how to talk to the kidsWhat you can skip, a note to her husband

Household things like "trash days" and "how to wash school shoes" sit in the same book as emotional things like "signs they're anxious." That alone really impressed me.

The key is that it doesn't just list times. The "Notes" column explains why. When you know the reason, as in "after 17:30 they'll ask you to pick him up," the person taking over can make their own decisions.

What she did really well

1. The first page lowers the bar

Page 1 opens with "You don't need to do everything perfectly." It says it's enough if the kids eat every day, sleep, and get to school and daycare. After that, it lists just 3 things that really must be kept.

The housework page also says "It's fine if the house is messy." It writes everything down, and then also says how much you're allowed to let slide. I think that takes a huge weight off the person receiving it.

2. The two kids sit side by side

Basic info, things to bring, meals, bedtime. Every table is laid out as "item × older son × younger son," with the two kids side by side. An elementary schooler and a daycare kid have completely different things to bring and different ways of contacting the school. Side by side, they're easy to compare, and you never wonder which kid you're reading about.

3. The contacts come with short notes

It's not just a list of phone numbers. Each one has a note next to it. "If there's a fever, call first before going in." "Teachers can't pick up in the morning because they're with the kids, so report absences through the form." There are plenty of times when that one line is more useful than the number itself.

4. It even covers feelings

What to do when they're in a bad mood, signs of anxiety (whining, saying their tummy hurts). It even gives the words to use: "Keep telling them that Mom is in the hospital to have the baby, and that she's definitely coming home."

There were little ideas too, like letting them pick a sheet of origami paper they like when they finish a full row on their study chart, and asking him to hand them a letter from Mom every day. These are things that only exist inside her head.

5. Medicine goes on a separate sheet

The medicine summary is 2 pages, separate from the handover notebook. It's split into each kid's oral medicine, medicine only for fevers, creams, and medicine the whole family can use, in 3 columns: "name, what it's for, how to take it."

Next to the difficult names printed on the medicine packets, she added plain words like "calms a cough" and "stops itching." With that, Dad can understand it right away too.

6. The blanks say "I'll fill these in later"

There are actually blank spots here and there. Next to them she wrote "I'll fill in the blanks before I go into the hospital" and "I'll fill in the actual times."

She used Claude to build the framework first, then filled in the contents herself. She didn't try to finish it all in one go. It's exactly what I said in the last post: don't try to write it perfectly from the start. She did just that.

The last page

The very end of the notebook has tips for talking to the kids. Then it closes with a few words for her husband.

It's okay if some days don't go well.
Just having you there
makes the kids feel safe.

* The real one has her husband's name in it.

I really loved that this line comes after 9 pages of tables. A handover guide is a to-do list, but I realized it can also be a letter that cheers up the person receiving it.

Summary

  • A 9-page handover notebook plus a 2-page medicine summary. 12 sections, from housework to feelings
  • Notes with reasons, tables with the two kids side by side, medicine on its own sheet. It's set up so the person receiving it never gets lost
  • "You don't have to be perfect" and "it's fine if it's messy": it also tells you where you can let things slide
  • Build the framework with Claude, then fill in the contents yourself

Thank you so much for showing me, and for saying it was okay to put this on the blog. Take it easy during these days with the baby.

If you have time away from home coming up, try giving Claude these 12 sections and saying, "Make one for our family."
