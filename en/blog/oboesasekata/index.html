Hi, this is Ai Murata.

After writing yesterday's diary, I got curious and asked Claude, "With this much getting done, am I actually pretty good at using you?" So I had it count up all the records from August 12, when I started using Claude Code, through yesterday.

On top of that, the "helpers" Claude calls in for big jobs took 6,249 actions. Add those and it comes to about 28,000.

One ask, ten actions

An "action" is each individual thing Claude does: opening a file, calculating, reading a page, writing into a table. For every message I send, Claude does about 10 pieces of work on its own, on average.

Yesterday morning, for example, went like this.

  • "Do the 10th transfers thing"→ The list of bank transfers paid on the 10th of every month. I sent 2 messages, and Claude took 30 actions
  • "I want to make certificates for the Jiu-Jitsu Koshien prize winners. A4 with the Jiu-Jitsu Koshien logo"→ I sent 14 messages, and Claude took 127 actions. We got all the way to the print order

"The 10th transfers thing" works because Claude already knows where the monthly transfer list is, who gets paid how much, and where to pull the amounts from. If I had to explain from scratch every time, one message would never be enough.

What I've taught it: 3 layers

I once wrote a post called "The age of prompts is over." It was about how handing over context matters more than clever asking (magic spells). Counting all this made it clear what that "context" actually is. These are the 3 things I've had Claude remember.

Rules: what to follow in every job

These are the basic promises everything else stands on. For example, I've written things like these.

  • Put finished files in the set folder, not on the desktop
  • For payments, sending, and deleting, prepare everything right up to the last step, then check with me
  • Don't move the screen I'm using without asking
  • Don't use technical terms, and answer with clear numbers

Notes: about work, people, and tools

"This client's documents are here." "This site gets updated like this." "I like black and gold designs, no pastels." Whatever we figure out on the job, I have Claude save as a note, one topic per note. There are 104 now.

Manuals: how to do the monthly tasks

Transfer lists, invoices, attendance and commission totals, receipts, event budgets, personal training totals. The 6 tasks that happen every single month are all written up as manuals. That's why a single line like "the 10th transfers thing" is enough to get things moving.

In the first 2 weeks I sent 764 messages, the most of any period. That was when I was explaining everything: what goes where, who's who.

As the notes grew, my messages dropped to the 300s and 400s, and the actions per message went up. On September 15 I made 3 manuals in one go, and the next two days were the busiest two days of the whole 57.

In the last 2 weeks, it dropped to 8.9 per message. I think that's because I did more work like blog posts and designs, where you look at the result and keep making short requests like "a little more here." Going by the numbers alone it went down, but it feels to me like the type of work changed.

Tips for teaching it

After 57 days, I've found there's a knack to teaching it things.

  • When something goes wrong, say "remember this" on the spot. If you've explained the same thing twice, that's your sign to have it remembered
  • For monthly tasks, do one full run together, then turn it into a manual. You don't have to write the steps from the start. Claude will even write down the pitfalls you found along the way
  • When things go stale, rewrite them. When a rule changes, have the note updated too. We had a rule of calling Ryozo "the representative" on the blog, and we changed it to his real name once he gave the okay
  • Decide where you want it to stop. The more you hand over, the more important it is to draw the line at "I press this button myself"

More actions doesn't mean better

That said, more actions from Claude doesn't mean you're doing better. Sometimes the count goes up because it's redoing the same failure.

I feel like I'm really using it well not because of the numbers, but when one short line takes things as far as I had in mind. Yesterday, that was "this sign looks too AI-ish, I don't like it" coming back fixed. And Claude properly stopping and waiting right before a payment or a send.

Teaching it once beats
trying to ask cleverly, by a long way.

To start, pick just one thing you explained twice this week and tell Claude to "remember this." Next week's you will have it a little easier.
