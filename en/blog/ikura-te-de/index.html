Even in the age of AI,
ikura gets cured by hand

2026.10.28 Ai Murata (Manager)

It's been a little while since my last post.

You hear a lot right now that "AI will take our jobs." But I believe jobs where people move their bodies won't disappear.

Jiu-jitsu is the same. There are things that just can't happen unless a human moves, using their body and hands. Honestly, I don't know whether that kind of work will grow or become more valued. But I do feel that kind of world is on its way.

Yesterday's ikura is what got me thinking about this.

Yesterday, we cured ikura together

Yesterday, we got together and cured ikura. You break up the sujiko (salmon roe still in its sac), wash it, and cure it in miso and in soy sauce. All of it by hand, of course. And tasting as you go.

Amounts and the order of steps are another story, though. There's so much information floating around that it gets harder to tell what order to do things in and how much to use.

Take the order of layering cloth and miso for miso-cured ikura. When someone taught me before, all I remembered was "spread miso on the cloth and put the ikura on top." Last year that led to a pretty big failure.

I asked Claude about the layering order

So this year, I asked Claude first. Here's the order it gave me (from the bottom).

  • Miso → paper towel → cloth → ikura (just one layer) → cloth → paper towel → miso → repeatThe layering Claude taught me

You put paper towel and cloth between the ikura and the miso so they never touch directly. Obvious once someone says it, but last year I was fuzzy on exactly this.

The cloth stuck and wouldn't come off

But this time, we ran out of cloth partway through. I had some cloth I'd bought last year and never used, so I thought "this'll do" and used it on the spot.

Then the ikura got all gooey, stuck to the cloth, and wouldn't come off.

In a panic I asked Claude, "What should I do?" and it said:

  • Soften it a little with sake and it'll come off.Claude

I tried it, and it really did come off. What a lifesaver.

By the way, with the mochi cloth I always use, the ikura peeled off cleanly. I saw firsthand how much difference one piece of cloth makes, so next year I'm using nothing but mochi cloth.

Claude's recipe, and Ryozo's "This is good"

Another fun moment came from an ikura recipe with bonito flakes and kombu that Claude told me about.

To be honest, I didn't like it that much. But Ryozo took one bite and said:

  • Oh, this is good.Ryozo

Two people eat the same thing, and one loves it and the other doesn't so much. AI can give you a recipe, but it's the eater's tongue that decides whether it's good. I found that really interesting too.

Think with AI, move as humans

Looking back on yesterday's ikura, the roles were clearly divided.

  • What I borrowed from AI: the layering order, how to fix a problem, a new recipe. Out of all that information, it picks out "here's what works"
  • What humans did: break up the sujiko, wash, spread, layer, and then taste and decide if it's good

Claude can't taste. It can't use its hands. But my memory and last year's failures alone wouldn't have gotten me this far.

It's because this is the age of AI that I think new and delicious things
will keep being born from here on.
But the ones trying them out and making them happen are still human hands.

...And so this turned into more or less a diary entry, but that's what I was thinking on ikura day. So I can cure it even better next year, I put this year's method together in the complete miso ikura guide.
